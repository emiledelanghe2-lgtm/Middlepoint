Resolving an argument with your partner: 6 steps that work

Written by the Middlepoint team, October 20, 2026.

The same argument keeps coming back and nobody remembers why. That happens in almost every relationship. The good news: it is usually not about ill will, but about how the conversation starts and how people listen. These six steps are simple, but they change a lot.

Why the same argument keeps coming back

An argument is rarely about the subject itself. Someone arguing about the dishes is usually arguing about something else: being seen, being appreciated, not being a burden, or getting some space. As long as you keep talking about the dishes, the real question stays untouched, and the argument returns.

That is why 'who is right' rarely works. You can both be right about the facts and still both end up disappointed.

Step 1: take a break when it gets too heated

Under stress we mainly listen for danger. You hear criticism, even when there is none. Do you notice your heart beating faster, or that you start shouting or going silent? Then calmly say that you need a break, and when you will come back. For example: 'I really want to talk about this properly, but I am too angry. Can we continue in an hour?'

Give yourself at least twenty to thirty minutes. Do something calming, and do not replay scenarios in your head. It is not running away, as long as you really do come back.

Step 2: start softly

The first sentence determines much of what follows. An accusation invites defense. Your own feeling invites listening.

Instead of: 'You never help.' Try: 'I feel alone with the housework and it is wearing me out. Can we look together at how to split this?'

A soft start has four parts: what is happening, how you feel, what you need, and what you suggest.

Step 3: summarize what you heard

Before you answer, repeat in your own words what the other person said. 'If I understand correctly, you feel unseen when I come home late. Is that right?' The other person can then correct you, and feels heard. That alone often takes a lot of tension away.

You do not have to agree. You only show that you understood.

Step 4: look for the need beneath the subject

Ask yourself and each other: 'What do I actually need here?' With the weekend at the in-laws, that can be 'connection with family' for one person and 'quiet time to recharge' for the other. Both needs are real. Once they are on the table, you can look for a solution that gives both of you something.

Step 5: make one small agreement

Do not choose a big promise like 'it will never be like this again'. Choose something small and concrete: 'This week I will cook on Tuesday and Thursday, you on Wednesday and Friday.' Small agreements get carried out, and they build trust.

Step 6: look back after a few days

After a few days, ask each other: 'What went better? What did not yet?' That keeps the agreement alive and nobody feels judged. What does work, you hold on to.

Please note. Is this about violence, threats or fear of the other person? Then an online conversation is not the right place. In the US you can call the National Domestic Violence Hotline for free at 1-800-799-7233, and in the UK the National Domestic Abuse Helpline at 0808 2000 247. In Flanders you can call 1712 for free. In case of immediate danger, call 911 (US), 999 (UK) or 112 (Europe).
